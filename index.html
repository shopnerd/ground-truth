1Course description

Ground truth is what surveyors call the check: you take the model, a map or a satellite image, and walk the ground to see whether it lied.

Landscape architecture now runs almost entirely on models. Terrain is a mesh, water is an arrow, a garden is a planting schedule, and AI will soon draw all of it faster than any of us. This course asks what gets lost between the model and the ground, and who, or what, notices. Its answer is that the gap is where art lives. A body pressed into earth, a sound recorded at a root, a ritual repeated until it changes the one who performs it: these register what the model cannot.

The working method is the score, a set of instructions that others carry out, in the tradition of Lawrence Halprin's movement notations and the event scores of Fluxus. Here a score is performed by many hands at once: a student, a tool, sand, water, light, sound, a seed, a fungus, the weather, and the passing public. No one fully controls the result. That loss of control is the lesson.

The course grows out of Aroussiak Gabrielian's practice: Future Kin, Tree Score, Transcorporeal Atmospheres, the Near-Extinction Rituals series, and the Landscape Futures Lab's Responsive Topographies. Its vocabulary is hers: attunement, conspiring, trans-corporeality, the hydrocommons, kinship, speculative yet functional.

2Learning outcomes

By the end of the term, students can:

  1. Attune: observe a ground with every sense and record what the model leaves out.
  2. Read the ground digitally: model real terrain, water and sun, and say plainly what the model assumes.
  3. Compose: write a score that people, tools and living matter can perform, and accept what the performance does to it.
  4. Read the sensor: know what a depth camera sees and misses, and design a tool whose marks it can and cannot read.
  5. Work with living matter: keep something alive for weeks, document it honestly, and own its death if it dies.
  6. Make time visible: use projection, time-lapse and sound to show change a drawing cannot hold.
  7. Stage an encounter: put a work in front of the public and let them change it.
  8. Argue from the ground: write about where the model and the world disagreed, and what that means for design.

3Three grounds

The course works on three real grounds at once. Two are distant deserts failing for lack of water. The third is a forgotten strip a block from campus that hundreds of thousands of people will pass during the 2028 Olympic Games.

4The instrument

Every score passes through one loop at the MLA's AR sandbox. Step through it, or press any stage.

5Schedule

The term is built like a piece of music in three movements: listen first, then compose, then perform.

Weeks 1–5

I. Attunement

Listen before you make. Matter before sites: soil, water, breath, the body. The sandbox as a listening device.

Weeks 6–10

II. Composition

Write for tools, light and matter. Hand tools, projection as weather, an AI to argue with, living tables.

Weeks 11–15

III. Performance

Stage the encounter. Rituals, ensembles, and the Maker and the Witness. A public night ends the term.

Use the left and right arrow keys to move between weeks.

6Assignments: six scores

Every assignment is a score: instructions that someone or something else performs. They grow from one student and one patch of ground to an ensemble in front of the public.

7The final: a public night

The course ends not with a jury but with a performance anyone can walk into, touch and change.

  • The night opens at dusk on the Exposition strip, where students perform their rituals of care for the people driving past. Without Metro's permission yet, the strip is performed in sand instead.
  • It moves a block to the MLA sandbox, where each ensemble performs its score live in sand, under projected light and sound.
  • Visitors press, dig and pour with the students' tools. The camera reads their marks and the app answers with water and shadow, so every visitor changes the piece.
  • The living tables stand on plinths with their five-week time-lapses and the app's predictions, the dead ones included.
  • At the end of the night the sand is raked flat by hand. Nothing is kept except a short film and each ensemble's two-voice statement, printed as a pamphlet.

Esther Margulies, Alex Robinson and Takako Tajima are invited as critics, walking through the night rather than sitting in a row.

8Reading, looking, listening

Readings are short. Listening counts as reading. Works to look at are studied with eyes, ears and hands.

    9Grading and policies

    Policies

    • Fab lab training is required before any tool is made in Score 3.
    • Living matter is kept, not abandoned. A death is documented, not hidden.
    • No one works on the Exposition strip until Metro gives written permission. The strip edges a live rail trench and a freeway exit.
    • The field journal is graded on honesty and attention, not polish.
